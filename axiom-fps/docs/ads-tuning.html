Tuning ADS

Aiming down sights is the one piece of weapon feel you can't judge from the inspector. The numbers on AFPS_StatsADSSettings don't tell you whether the optic sits centred, whether the gun clips the near plane on the way in, or whether the zoom lands before or after the weapon settles. Traditionally the only way to find out is to enter play mode, equip the weapon, hold aim, quit, change one number, and do it again.

The ADS preview removes that loop. It stages the weapon on the player rig in edit mode and drives the real cameras, so the Game view shows the true aimed framing — the same two-camera composite the player sees, not an approximation of it.

How the aimed pose works

Worth understanding, because everything below follows from it.

AFPS_WeaponSystem composes each weapon's local transform every frame from its resting offsets plus the ADS offsets. At full aim those cancel out, and the pose collapses to:

weapon.localRotation = inverse(aimPoint.localRotation)
weapon.localPosition = -(weapon.localRotation * aimPoint.localPosition)

In plain terms: the aim point is parked exactly on the weapon system's origin, looking straight down its forward axis. In the stock player prefab the weapon system sits at (0, 0, 0) under the camera, so the aim point lands on the eye and looks where the camera looks.

Two things follow:

  • You never position the weapon for ADS. You position the aim point, and the weapon is placed to satisfy it. The aim point marks where the shooter's eye goes, not where the optic glass is — the optic sits in front of it.
  • Alignment is structural. As long as the preconditions below hold, the sight cannot be off-centre. There is no per-weapon alignment to measure or fix, which is why the tooling validates structure instead of reporting offsets.

Previewing

Select an ADS component and press Preview ADS.

The weapon can be a prefab asset in the Project window or an object placed in the scene — including one parented under a container object, since the preview stages the object carrying AFPS_WeaponObject rather than the top of its hierarchy.

The preview stages a throwaway copy of the weapon under the weapon system, assigns it the weapon layer, applies the scoped-visibility swaps and drives the main and weapon camera FOVs. Look at the Game view, not the Scene view — the Scene view camera has its own FOV and doesn't render the weapon camera's composite.

Control What it does
Aim Blend Scrubs hip (0) to fully aimed (1). Use it to catch the weapon clipping the near plane or sweeping through geometry mid-transition.
Hip / Aim Snap to either end.
Play In / Play Out Runs the transition at the profile's real timings. Position, rotation and both FOVs advance on their own configured times, so this shows the actual lead-lag between the weapon settling and the zoom landing.
Frame Aim Point Points the Scene view at the staged aim point without selecting it, so the preview controls stay in the Inspector.
Centre Reticle Draws a screen-centre marker over the Game view. On by default; the setting is remembered between previews.

The Scene view also gets an ADS Preview overlay while a preview runs, with its own blend slider and a Stop button — see below.

The Scene view overlay

A preview outlives the selection — select a light, a piece of level geometry, or the aim point itself and the Inspector panel goes with it, while the preview keeps running with the cameras still zoomed.

So while a preview is active, an ADS Preview overlay appears in the Scene view with the weapon's name, an aim blend slider and a Stop ADS Preview button. It shows and hides itself with the preview, and like any overlay it can be moved, docked or collapsed from the Scene view's overlay menu.

The aim point handle is the one thing that does still need the ADS component selected, since it's drawn by that component's editor.

The centre reticle

The game's own crosshair is UI Toolkit and doesn't run in edit mode, so without a marker there is nothing in the Game view to judge the optic against. The preview draws its own: a four-segment cross with a deliberate gap at the exact centre, so the optic's reticle stays visible inside it rather than being covered by the marker meant to check it.

It's a screen-space overlay canvas on a throwaway object, like everything else the preview creates — it is never saved, and it disappears when the preview stops. Turn it off from the inspector if it gets in the way of judging a magnified scope.

Editing the aim point live

While the ADS component is selected and a preview is running, a handle is drawn on the staged aim point in the Scene view — it follows the active Move or Rotate tool. Drag it and the change is written straight to the real weapon's aim point; edit the real one and the preview follows. The sync runs both ways.

The handle is drawn rather than having you select the aim point outright, because selecting it would swap the Inspector away from the ADS component and take the preview controls with it. Frame Aim Point brings the Scene view to it without changing the selection.

Scene objects get a single undo entry per drag; a prefab asset edited this way is saved when the preview stops.

Note

While fully aimed, the aim point appears not to move when you drag it. That's the aimed pose doing its job — the aim point is pinned to the eye by definition, so what you're actually moving is the weapon, in the opposite direction. Drag the aim point up and the gun goes down. Scrub the blend below 1 if you'd rather see the handle move.

Correcting sight cant

The aim point's rotation is honoured as well as its position. Point it down the optic's true line of sight and the weapon rotates by the inverse to match. This is how you fix a model whose sights aren't square to its forward axis, without touching the mesh.

An aim point left at identity rotation behaves exactly as it always has, so existing weapons are unaffected.

What the preview leaves out

  • Bob, sway, recoil, tilt, the weapon bump and the equip animation. All are zero at rest, and none can be reproduced meaningfully without play mode.
  • The scope sprite HUD overlay, and the game's real crosshair. For a magnified optic, the framing you see is the weapon and the zoom, not the scope mask.
  • The scoped-visibility swap is applied at full aim rather than after the scope fade delay, since a scrubbed blend has no wall clock.

Nothing you author is modified. The staged weapon is flagged DontSave, so it can never be serialized into a scene or applied back to a prefab. Camera FOVs are the only writes to real components; they're restored on every teardown path, reverted around a scene save, and any prefab override the preview created is removed with them. The preview stops on play mode, script recompile, scene change and editor quit.

Validating setup

None of the ways ADS breaks produce a runtime error. They all just make the sight quietly land somewhere other than where you expect, which is why the checks exist.

The ADS inspector reports problems for the weapon you're looking at. Tools ▸ AFPS ▸ ADS Validation audits every weapon prefab in the project at once, plus the player rig — use it after any rig change, since a rig problem shifts every weapon simultaneously.

Weapon checks

Check Why it matters
ADS is on the same GameObject as AFPS_WeaponObject ADS resolves its weapon with GetComponent. On a child object it throws on the first frame.
Aim point is a direct child of the weapon root The aimed offset is -aimPoint.localPosition, which is measured against the aim point's immediate parent. Nest it under an optic child and the sight is off by that child's transform, silently.
Aim point isn't the weapon root itself Its localPosition would be the weapon's own offset from the weapon system, so aiming would fight its own placement.
Weapon root scale is (1, 1, 1) The aimed offset cancels the aim point's rotated local position, but scale is applied before rotation — so the two only agree at unit scale. Otherwise the aim point is stranded at (scale - 1) × localPosition instead of on the origin. Scale the model child instead.
camFOVAffect / weaponCamFOVAffect below their base FOVs AFPS_Camera applies them as a subtraction, so an equal or larger value drives the camera to a zero or negative field of view.

Rig checks

Check Why it matters
Weapon system is a child of the camera AFPS_Camera moves its own transform every LateUpdate. If the two aren't rigidly linked by the hierarchy, where the sight ends up relative to the eye can't be determined in the editor at all.
Weapon system is at the camera's position and rotation The aim point is parked on the weapon system's origin. Any offset there is an offset from the eye on every weapon.
Weapon system's world scale is (1, 1, 1) Equipping assigns transform.parent directly, which preserves world scale — so a scaled weapon system pushes every equipped weapon's local scale off 1, hitting the weapon-scale problem above.

FOV checks and rig checks both need a player in an open scene. Prefabs scanned on their own get the weapon checks only, and the window says so rather than silently reporting fewer problems.

Debugging a live aim

In play mode the ADS inspector shows a Live State panel: the input intent (Wants To Aim) separately from the applied state (Is Aiming), plus all four blends.

That separation is the useful part. A stuck or unresponsive aim is almost always one of two shapes:

  • Intent true, aiming false. Something is hard-cancelling: the weapon is holstered or switched away, a reload is running, or the player is mantling or ledge-hanging.
  • Both true but a blend never reaches its target. The transition time on the settings asset is the thing to look at.

The same blends are available to your own code as read-only properties on ADS (AimBlend, AimRotationBlend, CameraFOVBlend, WeaponCameraFOVBlend, WantsToAim, IsAiming), so a HUD can fade a reticle with the aim without duplicating the timing.
